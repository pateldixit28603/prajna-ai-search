## Who or what is Moon, and what are the key facts to know?

Moon is Earth's only natural satellite [1]. It orbits Earth in an elliptical path, averaging about 378,000 km away, and completes one orbit every 29.5 days [1]. The Moon is tidally locked to Earth, so the same side always faces us, and its phases cycle over a lunar month [3]. The first human-made object to reach the Moon was Luna 2 in 1959, and the first crewed landing was Apollo 11 in 1969 [4][7]. The Apollo program conducted six crewed landings between 1969 and 1972, with twelve astronauts walking on the lunar surface [7]. The Moon's diameter is 3,474 km, and its mass is 1.2% of Earth's [1].

## Matrix Multiplication is Θ(n^2.25) — what is this about and why does it matter?

The claim that matrix multiplication is Θ(n^2.25) refers to the best-known asymptotic complexity for multiplying two n×n matrices, meaning the operation can be performed in time proportional to n^2.25 up to constant factors [1]. This matters because matrix multiplication is a central subroutine in theoretical and numerical algorithms for numerical linear algebra and optimization; faster algorithms enable significant speedups in scientific computing, pattern recognition, deep learning, and other fields where these operations are fundamental [2][5].

The Θ(n^2.25) bound is not the historical schoolbook Θ(n^3) nor Strassen's Θ(n^log₂7 ≈ n^2.807). Instead, it represents the current best asymptotic bound discovered in recent research, improving upon earlier results. The progression of matrix multiplication complexity includes: [1]

- Schoolbook algorithm: Θ(n³) [1][2]
- Strassen's algorithm (1969): Θ(n^log₂7) ≈ Θ(n^2.807) [1][2]
- Subsequent improvements reducing the exponent further [2][5]
- Current best bound: O(n^2.371339) as of September 2025 [2]

The gap between the theoretical optimal complexity and practical implementations matters because algorithms with better asymptotic bounds often have large hidden constant factors, making them impractical for real-world sizes despite superior theoretical growth rates [5]. The development of faster matrix multiplication algorithms has direct implications for high-performance computing domains like deep learning, scientific simulations, and video graphics, where matrix operations form the computational foundation [6].

The pursuit of faster matrix multiplication remains a major open question in theoretical computer science, with recent claims of proofs formalized in proof assistants like Lean suggesting active progress toward resolving the asymptotic complexity [1].
